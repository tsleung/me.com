spread

why splitting a bet beats making it

one deck, one wager: is the next card red? bet even money and it's a coin flip. but you don't have to put the whole stake on one card. you can split it across many cards at once, and you can play round after round. those two moves — spreading a bet across hands and over time — are the whole of diversification, and a deck of cards shows exactly what they buy you. deal a few rounds by hand first; the charts further down are just what that looks like a thousand times over.

00 · the table

deal it yourself

this is blackjack stripped to its skeleton. no hitting, no dealer, no strategy chart — just one shared deck and a single feature to bet: is the card red or black? losing all of that complexity is the point, because it leaves the two things this app is about in plain view — how you split a bet across hands, and how the deck drifts as it empties.

each round you stake a slice of your bankroll and deal it across your hands — one card each — then the matching color pays, the rest don't, and the spent cards go to the discard. the next round deals from what's left, so the deck draws down as you play; when it can't cover a round, you reshuffle. deal a single hand and it's a coin flip you can feel; push the hands up and watch a whole row come out at once.

nothing here is a simulation — it's the actual game, one deal at a time. keep an eye on the deck's red/black split as it empties: that drift away from an even shoe is a real, exploitable signal, and in real blackjack it's exactly what a card-counter trades on. that betting strategy is coming; for now, just build the intuition — then the two chapters below zoom out to what happens over thousands of rounds.

01 · across hands

one hand, then two, then half the deck

you're staking everything this round. on one hand, you bet it all on a single card: you either win the payoff or you don't — a jagged, two-outcome gamble. split the same stake across two cards and there are now three outcomes; across twenty-six cards — half the deck in one pull — the count of reds barely strays from thirteen. the average payoff never moved. the swing around it collapsed.

drag the payoff up to its extreme — win doubles your money, a loss halves it — and deal: the single-hand distribution is two far-apart spikes, and every hand you add pulls the mass toward the middle. the second curve says why one deck beats betting on independent flips: the cards are dealt without replacement, so a red now makes a red later slightly less likely. that negative correlation is free variance reduction — and at fifty-two hands the whole deck is exactly half red, so the round is riskless.

02 · over time

the same bet, a hundred times over

now reinvest. each round you stake a slice of your balance, the deck reshuffles, and — as if paychecks kept arriving — fresh capital is injected on top. run a thousand parallel lives and two very different numbers emerge. the mean is what a whole room of players earns on average. the median is what the typical single player actually lives through. they are not the same number, and the gap is the point.

set the payoff to win-double / lose-half and bet it all on one hand: the average line climbs 25% a round while the median goes nowhere. a multiplicative bet that is a gain on average is a slow loss as you live it, because a doubling and a halving cancel to nothing — that's volatility drag. now raise the hands per round. each round's swing shrinks, the drag lifts, and the median rises to meet the mean. diversifying across hands is what lets you actually keep the ensemble's edge over time.

this split between the room's average and one player's fate is ergodicity: a multiplicative wealth process is non-ergodic, so the time-average a person experiences diverges from the ensemble average across people (peters 2019, nature physics; the ×2 / ÷2 coin is his canonical example). the optimal slice to stake is a kelly question — which the optimizer below works out for itself, along with a card-counter who turns the depleting deck into a real edge.

03 · the optimizer

let it learn what to bet

how much should you stake at each count? because the reward is log-growth, the answer is scale-free — it depends only on the deck, not your bankroll — so the whole policy fits in one table: for every composition of reds and blacks remaining, a single bet fraction. we don't hand it that table. an agent fills it in by playing, updating each cell online toward what it just earned, starting optimistically so every bet size gets tried before the estimates settle.

up top you watch it happen. the whole shoe is dealt out as columns of rounds — left played first, one card per hand — and the policy bets each round as the deck depletes and the count drifts. a bankroll runs underneath (take income every round, or pay to play — human capital cuts both ways), and every round's win-count drops into a histogram that bells into a normal as you add hands: the central limit theorem, the same diversification lesson, live. below it is the policy itself, learned online and sharpening toward the exact optimum beside it — and the counter's growth pulling away from the non-counter's, which is the cash value of information. change the payoff or the hands mid-run and the table re-adapts rather than restarting.

this is the reinforcement-learning skeleton of blackjack card counting, on the red/black toy: an action-value table over the count, populated by monte-carlo returns from an optimistic prior, read out greedily as a betting policy. the exact solver is here only to grade it — the agent never sees it.
